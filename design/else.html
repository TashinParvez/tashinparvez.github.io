01 — Origin

How I got here

Dhaka, Bangladesh · 2026

It started with curiosity, not a career plan.

I didn't wake up one day and decide to become a Product Manager. It started in university — I was deep into Computer Science, solving 1000+ competitive programming problems, and building systems for class projects. But somewhere along the way, I noticed something: I cared less about how the code worked and more about why we were building it in the first place.

The shift from code to product.

During my time as a Teaching Assistant, I realized I was naturally drawn to understanding what students actually struggled with — not just delivering content. I was doing user research without knowing the term. That instinct led me to my current role as a Product Analyst at Ontik Technology, where I translate user problems into structured requirements and work alongside engineers and PMs daily.

Where I'm headed.

I'm drawn to B2B and SaaS products — systems where the users are professionals, the workflows are complex, and good product thinking directly impacts business outcomes. I want to be in the room where trade-offs are made, roadmaps are debated, and ambiguity gets turned into clarity.

02 — Product Thinking

How I approach building

These aren't buzzwords. They're beliefs I've developed through real projects, failures, and hundreds of conversations with users and engineers.

01

Start with the problem, not the feature.

Most bad products exist because someone skipped the "why" and jumped to the "what." I always push to understand the user's actual friction before proposing any solution.

02

Ambiguity is the raw material.

Enterprise products live in messy contexts — conflicting stakeholder needs, unclear requirements, edge cases everywhere. I see ambiguity as something to structure, not something to avoid.

03

Ship learning, not just features.

Every release is a hypothesis. I'd rather ship something small that teaches us something than build something big that assumes we're right.

04

Documentation is a product skill.

Clear specs, structured requirements, and well-written PRDs aren't overhead — they're how cross-functional teams stay aligned without endless meetings.
